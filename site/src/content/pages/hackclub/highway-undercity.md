I think this was the moment where we really established that hardware at Hack Club could work long term. That it wasn't just some pipe dream, and that we could get a meaningfully make it work.

Highway was a program designed to level you up as a hardware builder, whether or not you even knew what hardware was. You could submit _any_ design, get up to $350 USD to build it, and also then get invited to Undercity, a hardware hackathon set to have 200 attendees.

Final stats:

- 16430 hours spent on finished, shipped hardware projects
- 845 projects approved & funded.
- 50+ countries
- 3 months of insane adventure.
- 166 attendees in-person in San Francisco

I co-ran this event with Acon, who was a fellow gap year at the time - their website is here: [https://acon.zip](https://acon.zip)!

Here's how it all came together.

_Fun fact about this one: Undercity actually started first! Highway was built almost entirely to be a funnel into Undercity._

---

## A bit of context

Acon & I had been thinking about running a hardware hackathon since around November / December. We weren't really sure about how this would be funded yet, as this predated the model of Hack Clubber projects subsidizing event costs - we knew it was something we wanted to do and were determined to figure out what the right pitch would be.

Planning at the start mainly comprised of throwing ideas on a doc and talking in passing between lunches. What type of badges should we have? What pieces of merch? What should people build? What city should we do it in? etc. Vaguely forming the shape of the event, but not quite a real plan.

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa1c-6c37-7457-aead-405e7ff1394b/paste-1793855351180.png)

<div class="caption">random picture of HQ, Jan 2025</div>

Fast forward to mid February, I was invited to dinner with [John Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cohn)! We talked about what our next steps were for hardware at Hack Club, as this was roughly right after Hackpad V1 had wrapped up and we were thinking about what was next.

We talked about running other programs similar to Hackpad (i.e make your own flipper zero clone, make your own mouse, etc), but we also started looking into what it would mean for Hack Club to run a _general_ hardware YSWS. Make anything you want, get the parts to build it. _How on earth would we do that?_

_I also want to add - it was so fun to just riff back and forth about projects and I got to see so many cool things._

I really want to set the scene for what the landscape of hardware at the org looked like at the time. Up until now, in the entire history of Hack Club, we hadn't really run a proper general hardware YSWS that consistently got new projects; we had Onboard, which gave teenagers a flat $100 to design assembled PCBs, but the range of options was somewhat restricted just due to how the program was set up.

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa1b-a383-7c41-8c35-93fedbe0e5ae/paste-1793855300162.png)

<div class="caption">here were your options as a Hack Clubber in Feb 2025!</div>

We didn't really conclusively get anywhere at the dinner, but it was nonetheless extremely helpful and helped the program feel more _real._ It set up a good set of problems for us to tackle later, such as:

- How do people actually get the parts to their designs?
- How do people get their 3D printed parts if they don't have a printer? Printing 200 hackpads was awful, I can't imagine what printing hundreds of different projects would look like
- How would the budget for this even work? How on _earth_ are we going to get the money for this?

So many problems. All the more exciting for us!

## Development

Hackpad V1 did really well, so naturally we decided to run a V2 right after, around the middle of February. While running that in the background, my attention was brought back once again to Undercity.

Undercity in particular had a couple of looming problems we had yet to answer:

1.  How was this going to be funded?
2.  Where were we going to host this thing?

For question #1 - going back and forth with Zach, the main requirement was that the program needed to result in weighted projects somewhat economically. This seemed like a good opportunity to run a general hardware program too. Make anything, come to hardware utopia. I think this was the moment we knew Highway was happening for certain.

For pricing, we ended up landing on $15/hr as a maximum for the all-in costs of the event, which meant that everything, including flight stipends, project funding, food, venue costs, etc ALL had to fit within $15 per hour spent by teenagers building projects to qualify.

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa1e-a1cc-7121-835f-2bb779c56d19/paste-1793855496485.png)

<div class="caption"> That being said, the target was still $10! Surely couldn't be too hard, right? </div>

For question #2 - that was a bigger question! We already had decided that we wanted to host this in SF for a multitude of reasons (Hack Clubber density, venue density, aspirational appeal, among others), but we were a little stuck on venue. Where on _earth_ were we going to host this thing? We originally wanted to aim for roughly 400 Hack Clubbers!

Getting venues is always a slog. I'll save you the trouble of reading, but long story short it was a LOT of emailing and asking around. SF Piers, Twitch, and Notion were among some of our considerations. Ultimately, it was GitHub that actually came through and helped make this happen! We ended up having weekly syncs with them up until the day of the event.

Soon enough another month passed and it was March, and Hackpad V2 fulfillment just about wrapped up. Once again we kidnapped a bunch of teenagers, and once again another 200 macropads were built & assembled and went out the door.

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa2f-377d-7743-b64b-e1a6d5df2a5c/paste-1793856582461.png)

Now it was time to get the ball rolling and figure out how we were actually going to get this off the ground, for real. Our time was running out - we wanted to run this thing by mid-July, and so we needed as much pre-event time as possible so that people could qualify.

First - we needed an actual team! We started roping in other members of HQ and active teens who had helped with previous programs. There was also now a mandatory Wednesday sync where we would keep track of things,

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa2e-c2a4-760b-86d0-54356a64150e/paste-1793856553439.png)

<div class="caption"> example update. </div>

Next, we needed an actual site design up! Acon put together a landing, and the whole team iterated on the figma back and forth. So, so many designs.

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa29-567a-740a-bd90-943deee03c89/paste-1793856197427.png)

 <div class="caption"> here were some drafts! </div>

In the middle of this, we were also trying to bang out the actual design of the hardware program. What were the actual final steps that someone would take? What were the final mechanics? I had written endless documents on mechanics I wanted to implement, but we didn't really have something precise. I put something together that was extremely platform-based with a signup flow and everything. This did not work; more on that later.

The final step after the flow & design were done was to just implement it! Internally within the org we just had an _awful_ experience using Next.js, so everyone was currently on the Ruby on Rails wave. We decided to opt for that too.

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa28-d26e-77a2-a91b-6fe720451f0d/paste-1793856164394.png)

<div class="caption"> I HATE YOU RUBY ON RAILS </div>

Small problem: nobody on the team knew Rails. This was before AI coding tools like amp, claude code, etc really existed either. So we couldn't patch it together. I distinctly remember being in Phil, one of the Hack Club buildings, and realizing that we were _not_ going to be able to implement the original platform we drafted. We ended up having to scrap the thing and basically scale Infill, the program I cobbled together in a weekend, to work with everything. Instead of a Rails backend we ended up just using Airtable automations for everything

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa27-0ebb-7f1e-a7b3-5ac5d2f948bf/paste-1793856048560.png)

<div class="caption"> airtable I could never hate you </div>

Anyhow, circling back a second, remember the budgeting issue we had? We never quite got around to solving that. Based on my projections from past programs + intuition I had a general sense that the budget _should_ work out in theory, but I couldn't really confirm this and we needed all the money we could get.

Zach and I had a couple meetings about how we might want to do this, and I ended up spending the entire week reaching out to people to see any bit of funding we could get. I didn't close anything but I did pull out feelers that will come in handy later.

I'll throw in that I was at TCT + Rapid in April, and while I was hoping to leverage connections from that for Highway, nothing really materialized from it. Super cool experience regardless and being there laid the groundwork for other things.

We ended up just hastily launching this. One of my core memories is me pulling us all in a room to make sure our email flows worked, and off to the races we were!

And on May 14th, 2025 and 6:30PM EST, we officially launched! Now the real work began

## Running the engine

The first thing to do after launching was to actually run Infill - which was May 15-17th, aka _the day after we launched_. Talk about bad timing. You can read about that event by going back one page! The only thing I'll throw in is that it was a _lot_ easier to close connections when I actually had Hack Clubbers around who could share their projects and tell their story. Many of the AMAs I ended up running were from this!

After that, I briefly returned home for a couple of weeks to Toronto. One thing that was deeply concerning to me was that the energy just didn't seem there. I decided to do a drastic measure

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa32-7f18-7a5d-9ff5-add9f28fad69/paste-1793856798247.png)

Pretty quickly, my entire week was just filled to the brim with user interviews.

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa34-64bb-7f63-bf7d-a035caa81328/paste-1793856922620.png)

<div class="caption">let me tell you. this was brutal</div>

Doing these interviews was _incredibly_ insightful and I sincerely wish I did them earlier! It really helped me understand what was going wrong with the program and it helped me steer it into a better position later on.

From here on out it was pretty smooth sailing for the next 2 weeks. It was pretty much just wake up, eat food, review projects, update docs and shoot emails, and go to sleep. Good times

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa47-4b9e-7b1b-89c4-d38b47aad063/paste-1793858160788.png)

<div class="caption"> sweet home Toronto. threw in a quick hack clubber meetup while I was there!</div>

I got back to HQ in early June. Around this time I was taking an uber back from HQ at 9pm or so when all of a sudden I got a message from a friend of mine that I was about to get on a call with someone from JLCPCB to talk about sponsoring & advertising Highway. We ended up closing $18k. Thank you JLC.

One thing I had to build post-launch was [Printing Legion](https://printlegion.hackclub.com), a p2p 3D printing platform comprised entirely of teenagers. That was a 2-hour vibecoding job. Still runs to this day!

Skip forward to the end of June, we were running the math and we realized that we weren't even on track to get 50 people there, much less the 200 we were targeting. _Fuck_, I thought, how were we supposed to get everyone in time? We were _very_ persistent with GitHub about fitting 200 people. It would be a crazy let down to have less than 50.

Clearly we needed to make it easier to qualify. Currently the system was earning points through making projects, and it required 8 points to qualify. What if we cut that in half? Would the math work out? Would we admitting more people than we could handle?

Well the cool part about math is that it actually _does_ work! Plugging in all our current metrics at the time produced this graph:

![image](https://cdn.hackclub.com/01a1aa3b-ef8d-7ed7-8727-f4435b5cd2d6/paste-1793857416458.png)

<div class="caption"> <a href="https://www.desmos.com/calculator/14giklmuec" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"> desmos </a> link here, if you're curious! </div>

And sure enough, the math just about works out to 166 people attending if we lower the requirements. And so we did.

I could go on and on about all the different things we did to get people to ship. From running AMAs & show n' tells, to short design challenges, to other things. For the sake of keeping this readable, I'll be skipping past a bit of it. Just know it was a LOT of work and patching.

I'll all say is that this progam was fun to run. It was one, crazy long, cathartic sprint.

## Undercity

Undercity was an entire other event being planned in parallel with Highway actually running, and was an entire adventure of its own.

I definitely need to update this section with more on how the hackathon itself actually came together and how we added in all the elements for that. Another time, perhaps.

Fast forward to the day of, we were letting people in and anxiously watching the attendee count. Our data was so discombobulated we didn't really know what our final attendee count was actually going to be. We were pretty certain it was going to be at least 130, but anything beyond that was a luck of the draw. As we anxiously watched the attendee count tick up though…

We ended up getting 166 on the dot. Turns out math really does work, huh.

Undercity itself was honestly a bit of a blur for me. I was profusely sick and went through 80 cough drops that weekend holding my body together. If I could tell Alex back then any advice, it would be to keep her head up and to make the most of it regardless. Whining gets you nowhere!

In the end, we ended up with 75 projects made across 166 teenagers. I recommend checking out the [Undercity Website](https://undercity.hackclub.com/)
